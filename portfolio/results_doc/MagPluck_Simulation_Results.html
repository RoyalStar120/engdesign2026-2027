Every result from our harvester simulation in one place: the hand calculations it checks, the redesign search, the time-domain runs, the frequency sweep, and the readouts from the interactive bench shown in our video.

What the simulation found

  • The poster's snap distances are about 2.4 times too small. Its own formula, dsnap = (4C/k)1/5, gives 13.3 / 14.3 / 15.3 mm, not 5.5 / 6.1 / 6.8 mm. With the right numbers the force margin falls from 12× to 0.37–0.49×.
  • Built as drawn, the magnets snap together and stay stuck at all three lengths. In the simulation the gap closes to about 2 mm and never opens again.
  • Our redesign plucks once every shake cycle with no contact. It uses a stiffer, shorter driver with a larger 12.7 mm magnet. The piezo beam is unchanged. Margin is 1.61–1.71× and the closest gap stays 1.44–1.50× the snap distance.
  • Output is 3.3 / 3.6 / 7.9 V peak for the 85 / 95 / 105 mm piezo beams with a realistic piezo coupling. That is far below the poster's 50 V target. The poster's 100 V/mm calibration would need more electrical energy than the beam stores.
  • Pull and release works: after the driver lets go, the piezo rings at its own frequency with 54–73% of the pull. But in steady shaking the next pluck arrives after only 1.1–1.6 piezo cycles, so the output stays at the shake frequency.

After the design review: five follow-up studies

  • Piezo on the driver (Section 10). The driver loses 2.2 mW to damping at 2 m/s² (105 mm). A second bimorph on the driver, placed where its strain stays under 500 µε, adds 340 µW, about 12× what the piezo beam makes. This is the biggest single improvement we found.
  • Design under uncertainty (Section 11). The paper's closed-form gain law is only reliable close to our current design. The exact linear two-mode model it comes from matches the full simulation (R² 0.90). Searching for the best median result, with assumed spreads in damping, stiffness, magnet strength, gap and coupling, gives a 105 mm design with a median power of 85 µW in the full simulation, against 9 µW for the current redesign.
  • Sweep-past magnets (Section 12). With small driver magnets sliding past the piezo magnet, the release is fast (0.05–0.34 piezo periods) and the piezo rings at about 95% of its pull. Steady output at 105 mm rises to 59 µW. Even so, the energy stays at the shaking frequency, so this is still not frequency up-conversion.
  • Recorded car vibration (Section 13). On a real cobblestone recording the magnets add 1.03–1.29× power. That is close to the paper's 1.35× for synthetic noise, and far below the 4–5× seen for a pure tone. The driver bimorph adds 2.2–2.6× on the same road.
  • First experiment (Section 14). A sweep at 0.1–0.5 m/s² is safe: the closest gap stays above 36 mm. The table gives the two mode frequencies, the voltages and the power gain we predict for each beam length.

Why steady shaking does not show the ring-down

Two things must be true for a visible pluck: the release must be quick compared with one piezo cycle, and the piezo needs several cycles before the next pluck. Raising the piezo frequency helps the second but hurts the first. Getting both would mean breaking the 1.5× snap rule or moving the piezo above the 18–30 Hz band.

10. Follow-up: a piezo on the driver beam

In the 105 mm design at 2 m/s², the shaking puts about 2.3 mW into the device, and almost all of it is lost as heat in the driver beam (2.24 mW in our model; the paper's audit gives 2.1 mW). The piezo beam turns only 28 µW of it into electricity. So we tested putting the same 40 × 5 mm bimorph on the driver too.

For each placement, the model:

  • recomputes the driver's stiffness as a stepped beam;
  • estimates its coupling from k31 and the bimorph's share of bending energy;
  • adds the bimorph's mass weighted by the bending shape;
  • re-tunes the dead tip mass so the driver stays at its design frequency;
  • drives the driver bimorph into its own matched load (about 1.3 MΩ for a 40 mm bimorph, more for shorter ones).

The catch is strain. The driver swings about 5 mm, and a bimorph at the clamp would bend to 1,000–1,700 µε, above the roughly 500 µε that PZT survives over many cycles. So the table shows two options for each length: at the clamp with no strain limit, and the best placement that stays under 500 µε.

What this means for the design

Even with the strain limit, a bimorph 10–30 mm from the driver clamp makes 200–340 µW, which is 10–80 times the piezo beam's output. The driver is where the energy is. The magnets still help the piezo beam a little, but a two-transducer design (or a single tuned beam with a transducer) would be the better design. The stepped-beam and coupling numbers are estimates, so a measured coupling on a real driver would be the first thing to check.

11. Gain law and design under uncertainty

The "gain" is the power with magnets divided by the power of the same beams with the magnets removed, at the driver's own resonance. Our paper's gain law (eq. 20) gives it in closed form:

G ≈ (1 + q²) · (Δ / (Δ − km))², with q = km / (2 ζd mbp ωd²)

Here km = 4C/de⁵ is the magnets' coupling stiffness and Δ = kp − mpωd². We first checked the law and the exact linear two-mode model it comes from against the full nonlinear simulation on 120 random designs from the search grid:

The closed-form law is fine near our current design (3.9 against 4.8 simulated for 105 mm). Across the whole design space it is often far too high, because it assumes weak coupling. The exact linear model is accurate (median error 14%), so we used it as the objective.

We then re-ran the design search with the gap safety factor as an extra variable (1.2–2.5). Each design was scored over 400 random draws, using the paper's spreads:

  • driver damping 0.015–0.05;
  • piezo damping 0.01–0.03;
  • both stiffnesses and the magnet constant ±7% (1σ);
  • gap ±0.5 mm;
  • coupling θ within a factor of 2.

A design is allowed only if its closest approach stays at least 1.2 times the snap gap in 95% of draws. The best designs were then re-checked on 48 draws with the full nonlinear simulation.

What changed and why

Both optimised designs use a longer, thicker, wider driver with the large magnet and a tighter gap (1.35× snap instead of 1.5×). This raises the magnetic coupling while keeping the closest approach above 1.2× snap in at least 95% of draws. The median gain at 105 mm rises from 1.9 to 4.4, and the median power from 9 to 56 µW (linear model).

Maximising gain and maximising power pick different designs. A high gain can come from a weak no-magnet baseline, so median power is the better target. The gain law on its own picks designs that the exact model rates much lower (row "max median gain-law value").

12. Sweep-past magnet geometry

In the current layout the magnets face each other along the shaking direction, so the pull fades slowly as the driver backs away. In a sweep-past layout the two magnets sit side by side across a small sideways gap, and the driver magnet slides past the piezo magnet. The force along the motion then rises, flips sign and dies within a few millimetres, so the release is fast.

Point dipoles are not accurate at a few millimetres, so we built a finite-size force model: each cylinder is split into 300 small dipoles. It agrees with the point-dipole formula far away and is lower up close, as expected for flat magnets:

  • the pull must be at least 1.5 × Freq;
  • the sideways attraction must stay 1.5 times below the beams' sideways stiffness;

Result

Sweep-past gives the "true pluck" the review asked for in a single pass: release in 0.22 of a piezo period and ring/pull 0.96, against 0.56 and 0.73 for the coaxial layout. It also roughly doubles or triples steady power (59 µW at 105 mm against 28 µW).

But with the piezo at 18–26 Hz the driver comes back before the ring builds up, so almost all the voltage is still at the shaking frequency and its harmonics. A 60 mm piezo beam at 61 Hz does ring between passes (about 8% of the energy off the shake harmonics), but it is so stiff that the magnets barely move it and the power collapses to 0.6 µW. True up-conversion needs a stiff, high-frequency piezo and a much stronger pluck, which this size of device cannot give safely.

The road spectrum is broad, with no strong peak at 16 Hz, so each resonance catches only a slice of it. The magnets add 1.03–1.29× on cobblestone (asphalt 1.16–1.36×), close to the synthetic-noise result (1.07–1.30×) and to the paper's 1.35×.

14. Proposed first experiment

This is the cheapest test of the model, and it is safe: a low-amplitude frequency sweep on a shaker. At 0.5 m/s² the driver tip moves only about 1.5 mm (105 mm design), and the closest gap stays above 36 mm.

  1. Ring-down first. Clamp each beam alone and flick it. Measure its natural frequency and damping from the decay (log decrement). Put these into the model before comparing.
  2. Sweep with magnets. Mount both beams on the shaker at the design rest gap. Hold a = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5 m/s² (phone or accelerometer on the base) and step 12–30 Hz in 0.1 Hz steps, waiting 5 s at each. Record the piezo voltage amplitude on a scope (1 MΩ input = our load).
  3. Sweep with dummy masses. Swap both magnets for non-magnetic slugs of the same mass and repeat. Same mass means the same frequencies, so the ratio is the gain.

15. Limits of the model

  • Each beam is one lumped spring–mass with one mode.
  • The point-magnet force law is less accurate closer than two magnet diameters. The redesign keeps the gap above that.
  • Section C's EI and the segment lengths are fitted to the poster, not measured.
  • The coupling θ is an estimate and could be off by about 2×.
  • Damping is 0.015 for both beams. PETG may be lossier (0.03–0.05), which would cut the driver swing.
  • Contact is a stiff spring, so stuck-magnet behaviour is only roughly right.
  • Not included: PETG creep, how the magnets are glued on, the riser block, and gravity sag.
  • The driver-bimorph coupling uses the same k31 energy-share estimate as the piezo beam, and the 500 µε limit is a common design value for PZT, not a measured one.
  • The sweep-past model ignores the beams' twisting and sideways motion (it only checks sideways snap), and it treats the two magnets as perfectly lined up along the beams.
  • The Monte Carlo spreads are judgements, not measurements. The car recording comes from a phone inside the cabin and has no mounting-point transfer function.
